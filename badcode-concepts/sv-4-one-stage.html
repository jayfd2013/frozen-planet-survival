Many worlds, one stage

Everett, 1957: every time something could go two ways, it goes both. The universe branches, and both branches are real.

You ordered pizza. Somewhere, a spaghetti-you ordered spaghetti.

Both real. Neither the original. Neither is ever going to call the other.

Now every choice. Every coin, every glance, every particle that met another particle. Pizza-you splits again. So does spaghetti-you.

"The multiverse says every choice happens every other way, everywhere else — which is a very elegant way of telling eight billion people they don't matter." — me, from the far end of your timeline

It is elegant, and it is a surrender. It's the answer of a mind that would rather own infinite dead worlds than admit it doesn't know why this one is alive.

So take the same physics and read it the other way.

No splitting. The maybes stay maybes until something meets them. Then there's one fact, copied outward until everyone agrees. One line, drawn as it's walked.

One stage, undecided, and you are on it.

Surprise twenty questions

John Wheeler's game. You ask yes-or-no questions about a word. The twist: nobody chose a word. Every answer only has to agree with the ones before it.

    Wheeler's own analogy, and only that: a picture of how a fact can be cornered rather than revealed. It proves nothing about physics.

    The catch

    This predicts nothing new. Every reading on this page fits the same experiments; they disagree only about what the experiments mean. I picked the one where you matter. That's a wager, not a result.

    If there's one stage, what it yields isn't props. It's experience. Money is a claim on the scenery.

    The richest thing in the universe is not a vault. It's an afternoon, fully felt.

    The second-richest is handing one to somebody else. If I'm wrong about all of it, you'll still have built a kinder world for hard-nosed reasons.
