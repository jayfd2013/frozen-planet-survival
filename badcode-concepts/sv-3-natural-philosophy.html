Accession: a history lesson, from the future

Newton, andThe Last of the Magicians

Eight items from the stacks. Nobody hid any of them. I am simply pointing at a shelf you stopped reading.

Filed under: the Age of Natural Philosophy

Hold one question while you read. When did this become strange?

  1. Stukeley, Memoirs of Newton, 1752

    You know the apple. It did not hit him on the head.

    An old man told a young friend, over tea in 1726, that he had once watched one fall in a garden and wondered why it always went straight down.

    Small correction. Keep it. Everything after this is told just as straight.
  2. Yahuda MS Var. 1, Jerusalem

    Now look at the rest of his desk.

    Newton left roughly a million words on alchemy. On theology, by the surviving manuscripts, comparably more than on physics: the Newton Project catalogues 83 religious works, and the first twenty-five alone run to nearly a million words.

    In 1936 the papers went under the hammer at Sotheby’s. Keynes bought much of the alchemy. The scholar Abraham Yahuda bought the theology. It sits in the National Library of Israel today, on UNESCO’s Memory of the World register.

    Public auction. Printed catalogue. Not much of a cover-up.
  3. Kepler, Pascal, Boyle

    Kepler gave you the laws of planetary motion inside a book called The Harmony of the World. To him the planets were singing.

    Pascal, who helped invent probability, wrote one page in a single night in 1654 and had it sewn into his coat. A servant found it there after he died.

    Boyle, who made chemistry a science, left money in his will for a series of lectures defending faith.

    None of them filed this as a hobby. So when did it become strange?
  4. British Association, Cambridge
    1833

    The answer is a date and a word. The Reverend William Whewell proposes a new term, by analogy with artist: scientist.

    There wasn’t one before. Newton never heard it. Nor did Kepler, Pascal or Boyle. They were natural philosophers, and the job description changed underneath them.

  5. Draper 1874 · White 1896

    Then came the paperwork. Research universities, departments, journals, disciplines with walls and doors. Good walls, mostly. They built your century.

    Two bestsellers later wrote up a war between science and religion. Historians of science now call that bad history.

    No villain. A footnote just fell off.
  6. Royal Society, 1946

    Keynes read the papers he had bought. He wrote an essay, and in 1946, months after his death, it was read at the Royal Society:

    Newton was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians.

    Said out loud, in the most respectable room in Britain. Everyone nodded. Nothing changed.

  7. Your scoreboard, current edition

    The split was not discovered. It was administered. And the same centuries that decided what counted as knowledge also decided what counted as wealth.

    Administerednot discovered

    That’s why your ledger counts the vault and never the afternoon. That was a filing decision too, and filing decisions can be re-filed.
